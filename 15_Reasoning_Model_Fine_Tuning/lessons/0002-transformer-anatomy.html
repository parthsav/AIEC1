Lesson 0002 · Transformer Anatomy

What Is LoRA Actually Patching?

Lesson 0001 said LoRA sticks a note onto "a few key weight matrices." This lesson answers: which matrices, and what do they do? You'll need this to answer Question #2 in your notebook, which asks you to label a real architecture diagram using your model's own print(model) output.

Analogy first Imagine you're telling a friend a made-up story out loud, one word at a time — you don't know the next word until the moment you say it. Before every new word, you quickly glance back over everything you've already said, think for a second, then say the next word. That's it. That's basically all a model does, five small habits repeated over and over. Let's watch it happen, one word at a time, using one sentence: "Emma had 3 cats. She..."

Click through the steps below — each one is a single habit in that process.

Step-through — one word of the story

1. Make a private note. You don't keep the raw word around — you keep a quick mental note of what it means and why it matters. The model does the same thing, in numbers instead of words. This is called embed_tokens.

2. Glance back at what you already said. Before picking the next word, you check your own earlier notes for what's actually relevant. About to say "She"? You check back and land on "Emma" — that's who you mean. The next step unpacks exactly how that "glancing back" works.

3. What "glancing back" is really doing. Every note has a label (what it's about) and content (the detail). Your question gets compared against every label to find the best match, then you actually read that note's content and blend it into one clean output. Four jobs, four names: Query (q_proj) asks, Key (k_proj) is the label, Value (v_proj) is the content, Output (o_proj) is the clean blend — the same four names you patched with LoRA in Lesson 0001, and together they make up self_attn.

4. You can't peek at words you haven't made up yet. You're inventing this story as you go — word 5 doesn't exist until you say it, so there's nothing to glance back at. That's the whole idea behind "masked": not a restriction bolted on, just an honest fact about making things up one word at a time.

5. Take a beat to think it over, alone. After glancing back, you privately mull over what you noticed — no more checking other words this time, just processing it on your own. This habit is called mlp. The next step unpacks exactly what that private mulling-over involves.

6. Expand, highlight, compress. up_proj blows your note up onto a bigger scratch pad — more room to work with. gate_proj highlights which parts are actually worth keeping (a "gate" lets some information through and blocks the rest, like a dimmer switch). down_proj then condenses the highlighted result back down to normal size before you move on. Three matrices, one job each — the other three names you patched with LoRA in Lesson 0001.

7. Repeat, then finally speak. You do "glance back, mull it over" many times in a row (28 times in this model) to really refine your idea — each round wrapped in a quick tidy-up step — before you actually say the next word. That last step is lm_head.

Step 1 of 7

Reading your own model's printout

When Task 3 runs print(model), it dumps the real class names for each habit above. Every Llama-family model (yours included) uses this same naming pattern, repeated once per round:

typical Llama decoder block, as it appears in print(model)
(self_attn): LlamaAttention(
    (q_proj / k_proj / v_proj / o_proj): Linear(...)   ← glancing back, query/key/value/output, masked (steps 2–4)
)
(mlp): LlamaMLP(
    (gate_proj / up_proj / down_proj): Linear(...)     ← expand, highlight, compress (steps 5–6)
)
(input_layernorm): LlamaRMSNorm()                       ← tidy up before glancing back
(post_attention_layernorm): LlamaRMSNorm()               ← tidy up before thinking it over

Outside the repeated rounds, near the top and bottom of the printout, look for embed_tokens (step 1 — making the private note) and lm_head (step 7 — finally speaking). Question #2's example already gives you the three LlamaRMSNorm() lines for free — your job is to find and label the other four using the names above, inside your own printout.

Where "query / key / value" comes from These names are borrowed straight from database vocabulary — the same key-value idea behind a Python dict, or a vector database from earlier sessions. A query is what you're searching for; a key is what you match against; a value is the content sitting behind a matching key. "Output" is simpler — it's just the tidy result after blending the matched values. This isn't a setting you choose: q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj are four real weight matrices baked into every attention block of every transformer-based model (GPT, Llama, Mistral — all of them), run every single time, no exceptions. gate_proj/up_proj/down_proj (step 6) are three more, specific to Llama's "expand, highlight, compress" style of feed-forward layer — 4 + 3 = 7 total, exactly the length of Task 3's target_modules list. Not a coincidence: that list is every projection matrix in one block.
Where this lives in your notebook Task 3 — the last line of the cell is print(model). Scroll its output slowly; the pattern above repeats 28 times (once per decoder block) with only the layer number changing.
