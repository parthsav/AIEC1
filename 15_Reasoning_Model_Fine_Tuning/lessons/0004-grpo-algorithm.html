Lesson 0004 · The GRPO Algorithm

Grading on a Curve, Instead of Hiring a Second Grader

Lesson 0003 said "the policy shifts toward what scores well." GRPO's whole trick is how it decides what counts as "well" — without a separate grading system.

Analogy first A teacher gives the same pop quiz to 8 students. Instead of comparing every answer to a fixed, official rubric written by a second expert (expensive to build), the teacher just grades on a curve: whoever scored above the class average did "well," whoever scored below did "poorly" — relative to their own group, on their own quiz. GRPO does exactly this, once per training step, for a group of 8 completions to the same question.

Reward vs. advantage — the distinction that matters

A reward is a raw score (Lesson 0005 shows exactly how it's computed). An advantage is that score compared to what was expected — and it's the advantage, not the raw reward, that actually drives the update. Classic RLHF (PPO) estimates "what was expected" with a second neural network called a critic, trained alongside the policy. GRPO skips the critic entirely: the group's own average is the expectation.

Illustration — 8 completions, one group average

Each bar is one of the 8 completions num_generations = 8 samples. Anything above the dashed group average gets a positive advantage (nudge toward); anything below gets pushed away. No second network ever judged these — the group graded itself.

PPO-style RLHF

Needs a trained critic network to estimate the expected reward for a baseline. More moving parts, more memory, more to get wrong.

GRPO (this notebook)

The group's own average reward is the baseline. No critic, no extra network — just sample more completions per question.

The leash: why there's still a KL penalty

Without a limit, a policy chasing reward can swing wildly in one update — sometimes even finding a cheap trick that scores well without actually reasoning better ("reward hacking"). GRPO adds a small penalty that keeps each update close to where the policy started, like a leash: room to explore, but not room to bolt.

Illustration — the KL leash

Reward pulls the dot rightward every step. The KL penalty is the leash holding it back from moving too far, too fast, in any single update.

Where this lives in your notebook num_generations = 8 in Task 6's GRPOConfig is the group size from the illustration above. TRL's GRPOTrainer applies a KL penalty by default even though this notebook doesn't override its weight explicitly — see the GRPOTrainer docs for the exact default. max_grad_norm = 0.1 in the same config is a second, simpler stability guard — it caps how big any single update step can be, regardless of the KL term.

Quick check: why doesn't GRPO need a separate critic network?

Primary source DeepSeekMath, Section 4.1, is where GRPO was formally introduced — it has the exact advantage formula (each reward, minus the group mean, divided by the group's spread). DeepSeek-R1 is where this exact recipe was scaled up to produce a full reasoning model.
