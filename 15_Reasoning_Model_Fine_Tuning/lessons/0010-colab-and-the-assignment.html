Lesson 0010 · Colab & The Assignment

Why Colab, and What You're Actually Being Asked to Do

Everything so far explained the notebook's ideas. This lesson steps outside the notebook, to the practical stuff: what Colab is, why this specific session needs it, what the assignment actually wants from you, and a first pass at the 4 graded questions.

What is Colab?

Analogy first Normally, running code means using the computer sitting in front of you. Google Colab is a computer you rent by the hour, that lives in Google's data center, which you control entirely from a web page. You type code into your browser; it actually runs on Google's machine, not yours. The appeal here: Google's machines can have a real NVIDIA GPU built in — yours might not.

Colab (Google Colaboratory) is a free-to-start, pay-for-more service that gives you a Jupyter notebook (the same .ipynb format you've been using) connected to a cloud machine instead of your laptop. You pick a "runtime" (which hardware you want), and Colab hands you one, temporarily.

Every other session in this course, you were just calling an API — any computer works. This is the first session that trains weights, and training needs hardware your Mac doesn't have. Colab is the workaround, not extra credit.

The activity, simplified

Stripping away every code detail, here's the actual ask, straight from the README's "Main Assignment" section, in plain words:

One sentence Take a chat model that's just okay at math, and use reward-based training — grading its own self-generated attempts against a hidden answer key, never training it to copy a worked solution — to make it noticeably better at solving grade-school math problems and showing its work. Then prove it actually worked by comparing its answers before and after, and demonstrate you understood why it worked by answering 4 short questions.
Wait — doesn't GSM8K give us the answers? Isn't that "showing examples"? Good catch if you're asking this. GSM8K gives the model a question — every training method needs that — and gives the reward function a hidden gold answer, used only to grade the model's guess afterward. The model itself never sees that gold answer; look at Lesson 0008's correctness_reward_func(prompts, completions, answer, **kwargs) — answer is received by the grading code, never passed to the model. What's genuinely missing is a worked solution to copy — that's the one thing supervised fine-tuning would show and GRPO deliberately doesn't. Think closed-book exam: you get the question, you write your own answer from a blank page, and only afterward does someone check it against an answer key you never had access to.

Question #1 — double quantization, and why 16-bit here

Asks: what gets quantized a second time in QLoRA, how much memory does that save, and why does this notebook use 16-bit instead?

Draft: Normal quantization needs small "scaling constants" to reconstruct the compressed numbers accurately — one constant per small block of weights. Double quantization compresses those constants themselves a second time, saving roughly 0.37 bits per parameter (this exact figure is stated in the notebook's own Task 2 text). This notebook skips QLoRA anyway and trains 16-bit, because GRPO's real bottleneck is generation speed, not memory — every 4-bit weight has to be decompressed before the GPU can use it (Lesson 0001's zip-file idea), and that tax adds up fast when you're generating 8 completions per question, every step. QLoRA is still the right call when GPU memory itself is the hard limit, not generation speed.

Question #3 — what LoRA is doing, in your own words

Asks: a plain description of what LoRA does.

Draft: LoRA freezes the model's original weights completely and instead trains two small matrices, A and B. Multiplied together, they form a small correction the same shape as the original weight, and the model actually uses original weight + correction. Only A and B ever get updated — a tiny fraction of the model's real size — which is what makes fine-tuning this cheap.

Question #4 — warmup_ratio, learning_rate, lr_scheduler_type

Asks: describe what these three training settings do.

Draft: learning_rate controls how big each update step is — too high overshoots, too low crawls. warmup_ratio is the fraction of training spent easing into the full learning rate gradually at the start, instead of applying it at full strength immediately, which keeps early training stable. lr_scheduler_type = "cosine" describes the shape of the learning rate over the whole run — it ramps up during warmup, then eases back down smoothly along a cosine curve toward the end, rather than stopping abruptly.
