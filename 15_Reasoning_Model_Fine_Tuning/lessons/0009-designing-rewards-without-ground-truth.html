Lesson 0009 · Designing Rewards Without Ground Truth

What If You Don't Know What "Good" Is?

Every reward function so far checked against something exact: GSM8K's gold number. Most real tasks aren't that lucky. This lesson is about what to do when there's no clean answer key to check against.

Analogy — a callback to the food critic (Lesson 0003) What if there's no food critic at all, and you've never eaten a genuinely great version of the dish yourself — you have no idea what "good" tastes like? You're stuck... unless someone can still tell you "I liked plate A better than plate B." That one comparison, repeated many times, is enough to learn from — even without ever writing down what "good" means in the abstract. That's the whole trick this lesson is about.

The spectrum: how exact does your reward need to be?

Illustration — from exact ground truth to no signal at all

You don't need to be all the way on the left. Most real tasks live in the middle three stops — and that's still enough to train on.

The trick in the middle: comparing beats scoring

Humans are bad at absolute scores ("rate this joke 1–10") but good at comparisons ("which of these two is funnier"). RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is built entirely on that one observation:

  1. Show people pairs of model outputs, ask which one is better.
  2. Train a small model — a reward model — to imitate that preference on new pairs it hasn't seen.
  3. Use that trained reward model as the automatic scorer, exactly where correctness_reward_func sat in your notebook.

This is literally how ChatGPT was first trained: nobody wrote a formula for "a good, helpful reply." People just compared pairs, and a reward model learned to imitate their preferences.

Your guitar tabs idea, revisited

Notice the "does resynthesized audio sound similar" reward from Lessons 0007–0008 already lives on this spectrum — it's the imperfect proxy stop, not the exact-ground-truth stop. You never claimed to know the "perfect" tab; you just built something checkable that correlates with good. That's normal, and it's usually enough.

The catch: reward hacking Whatever proxy you pick, the model will find every loophole in it, not just the path you intended. If "sounds similar" is the whole reward, the model might discover some acoustic trick that scores well without producing an actual playable tab. This is why the notebook stacks multiple reward functions (format + correctness together) instead of trusting one — closing loopholes is most of the real skill in reward design, more than picking one clever measure.

The honest limit

If truly nothing — no rule, no human, no model — can tell a good attempt from a bad one, even roughly, this whole family of techniques (GRPO, RLHF, all of it) has nothing to climb toward. That's a real boundary, not a failure of imagination. It's rare in practice — almost every task has some usable signal once you stop insisting on a perfect one — but it does exist, and it's worth recognizing rather than forcing a bad proxy onto a task that has no honest signal at all.

Primary source Christiano et al., "Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences" (2017) — the original paper behind the pairwise-comparison trick. Ouyang et al., "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" (2022, the InstructGPT paper) — how this was actually applied to build the model behind ChatGPT.
💬 Have a task in mind where you're not sure what "good" even means? Describe it to your teaching agent — figuring out where it sits on this spectrum is usually the fastest way to see if it's trainable at all.
