Lesson 0006 · Preference Pairs

Same Verdicts, Two Different Lesson Plans

Analogy first A coach who watched the same shooter take a group of free throws could teach two different ways. Way one: tell the shooter their average for that round, and let them adjust — that's what Session 15's GRPO did with the group average. Way two: pull out one good shot and one bad shot from the exact same round, put them side by side, and say "do more of this, less of that" — a direct, paired comparison. Way two is a preference pair, and it's what this lesson builds.

Everything so far — the policy, the verifier, the audit trail — has been building toward one question: what do you actually do with a pile of verified, audited samples? The answer is: two different things, from the exact same data.

Building a pair: winners cross losers, within a group

Code — Task 7: preference pairs

def build_preferences(groups: list[list[Sample]], records: list[dict]) -> list[dict]:
    """Cross verified-correct (unflagged) winners with incorrect losers, per group."""
    flagged_responses = {r["response"] for r in records if r["suspected_hack"]}
    pairs = []
    for group in groups:
        winners = [s for s in group if s.verified_correct and s.response not in flagged_responses]
        losers = [s for s in group if not s.verified_correct]
        pairs.extend(
            {"prompt": winner.problem, "chosen": winner.response, "rejected": loser.response}
            for winner in winners
            for loser in losers
        )
    return pairs
Python patterns you'll see here {r["response"] for r in records if r["suspected_hack"]} is a set comprehension (curly braces, no key:value) — a fast lookup table of every hack-suspect response text. The nested generator inside pairs.extend(...) is a double loop written inline: for every winner, for every loser, build one pair — this is the "cross product" mentioned in the notebook, and it's why one group of 4 with 2 winners and 1 loser produces 2 pairs, not 3.

Worked example — one group becomes two pairs

Recall the group from Lesson 0003: 3 correct, 1 incorrect. Now suppose one of those 3 "correct" samples got flagged by Lesson 0004's hack detector.

Sample 1 — winnerCorrect, not flagged → eligible as chosen
Sample 2 — winnerCorrect, not flagged → eligible as chosen
Sample 3 — excludedCorrect, but flagged as hack-suspect → NOT eligible as chosen, and not a loser either. Simply dropped.
Sample 4 — loserIncorrect → eligible as rejected

2 winners × 1 loser = 2 pairs: (Sample 1 chosen, Sample 4 rejected) and (Sample 2 chosen, Sample 4 rejected). Sample 3 contributes to neither — if a hack-suspect response were allowed as "chosen," the next policy iteration would be directly taught to hack more, which is exactly the failure mode Lesson 0004 exists to prevent.

Same verifier, two consumers

DPO (new this session)

Consumes {prompt, chosen, rejected} pairs directly — no RL rollout during training, just "make chosen more likely than rejected." This is exactly the schema TRL's DPOTrainer expects.

GRPO (Session 15)

Skips the pairing entirely and uses each sample's raw reward, compared to its group's average — the loop you already trained against in Session 15.

Both start from the identical thing: a verified, audited group of samples. The only difference is what you do with it next.

What we built, start to finish

  1. A policy — small, sampled at temperature 1.0, deliberately sometimes wrong (Lesson 0001)
  2. A verifiable domain — math with ground truth + \boxed{} (Lesson 0001)
  3. A reward function — deterministic, normalized, asymmetric (Lesson 0002)
  4. The sampling loop — groups, the same structure GRPO needed (Lesson 0003)
  5. Adversarial thinking — hack detection, an append-only audit trail (Lesson 0004)
  6. A second domain — code judged by unit tests, fractional rewards (Lesson 0005)
  7. Training data — audited samples become preference pairs, hack-suspects excluded (this lesson)

The one idea underneath all seven steps: wherever a deterministic program can check correctness, cheap inference becomes training signal — no human labelers, no learned reward model. The verifier's quality is the ceiling on the policy's quality.
